I purchased a marble run kit from Rokr. I had seen one at a metalworking expo and desired to see how difficult the assembly was. Two grandsons are at an age where they might have an interest. It is a nice project from the standpoint of time spent, which is whenever I have a few minutes to spare from the many other ongoing projects.

I am quite impressed by the quality of the work, both the design and the laser cut parts mostly in wood, but a few of plastic. The instructions were poorly translated as might be expected (i.e. polish instead of sand), but the pictures are similar to Lego instructions and the text is minimal.

Removing the parts, some small and some large, is done at the beginning of each step, with clear labeling to define the parts needed for the step. Removing each piece from the background can be a bit tricky, so I take my time, using a lot of wiggling motions to free the pieces. The attachment points then need to be sanded with the included sandpaper and sanding block.

Pressing the parts together is the most demanding and finicky part of the process. Parts are held together by squeezing one piece between two tabs on a second piece. On three occasions, while assembling the first four sections of track, an outer layer of the plywood popped off. These were easily glued back in place after the parts were together using cyanoacrylate glue and an accelerant. So far only one part has broken during assembly and the same treatment with glue quickly put me and the part back in business. A very patient ten plus year-old child could manage this assembly, one of my older grandsons fits these requirements.

When pressing the pieces together, I make sure my fingers, or in some cases tweezer ends, are opposite one another and on either side of the joint. This minimizes any stress applied away from the joint. My fingers are fat relative to the parts, so the tweezers have been very helpful.

The last subassembly constructed has been bothering me. All of the light brown stubs in the photo below are the same except one, the second from bottom on the right. This stub has a wider area near the main up-down line of the part. This prevents the dark brown rail from fitting close and leaving it at an angle, unlike all other parts joined. I assume this is purposeful and have moved on.

It was not clear to me what the dark brown I-shaped piece sitting on the lighter brown part was supposed to align with. Not seen in the photo is a second similar part. Both can slide up and down in the slot. Turns out both are there for later attachments and keep the long tan part from collapsing during pressing the two dark grommets, seen just outside the clear acrylic pieces. Once used they were removed.

A subassembly this morning required a C-shaped part fit around a flat board. The part had to be started at an angle and the opposite side of the board then pushed down into place. The fit was very tight and the C-shaped part broke. It was glued once the parts were held in place and is seen below after gluing.

This broken part and many others have extras provided on a sheet labeled spare parts.

One of the challenges presented by this type of construction is seen in the photo below. The curved rail needs to slip into its supports at 90° to one another. This means that while sliding the part onto one support the other support cannot be aligned and the part goes on at an angle. It is necessary to twist the rail somewhat to get one support to fit and then align the second.

By the end of today's well of patience over fifteen subassemblies have been completed. The wooden gears seen below must be waxed. An old block of parafin wax was used for this purpose. In the past this block of wax has only been used to lubricate wood screws. A block of wax was supposed to come with the kit, but was never located.

The project sat for months waiting for an opportunity for a grandson with a potential interest in the assembly to come over and explore. He likes the project and jumped right into the construction. Many parts were completed and the first subassembly was put together. This subassembly holds the crank which is used to raise the balls to the top of the run.

Returning to this project in October I need to fix a problem with the crank, it locks up on occasion. The crank locks up at times and the assembly has a lot of flex. The latter may be fixed when it is attached to some sort of base. The former was harder to identify, but at least one cause for locking up was identified. The plastic "gear" catches and this occurs when the flex pulls a part out of alignment. The photo below shows the location of separation, the small piece with the oval cutout right in the center of the picture is pulled from its point of attachment on the left.

This may need to be glued in place as it does not hold even when pushed together tightly. Gluing will await a better understanding of how this subassembly is supported. Minimizing the flex should fix the problem.

It is the beginning of November and a more beautiful day cannot be imagined, though today's high will be 20° above the highs later this week. A short trip to visit family in Vancouver just finished. What a wonderful city! A break from the knurling tool is in order and I need to finish this marble run to free up room for other projects.

The flex in the ball raising mechanism was repaired with superglue. The loose parts were held in place and a few drops of super glue were put on the joints. The pieces are now solidly held in place and the wheels turn easily with no sticking. Work changed from making various subassemblies to attaching subassemblies to the base. A wall filling most of one side of the base was built of plastic and wood parts as seen below. This wall was further adorned with parts on the face of the wall and on the top. The most challenging procedure in this whole build, so far, was putting small transparent plastic washers on shafts. These can be seen upon expanding the first photo below. Very tight!

Following this first wall the subassembly with the wheels and crank was locked to the base and attached to the first wall. Many small parts were then added to this crank assembly to provide attachment points and to increase its solidity. The crank was turned every so often just to make sure everything continued to function smoothly.

With two outside walls complete the process turned to utilizing the subassemblies already constructed, beginning with the lowest level of the ball return and proceeding to the second level from bottom as seen in the two photos below. Some very recalcitrant parts were run into, refusing to fit in their provided holes, but with careful use of needle nose pliers all were convinced to fit.

The spiral was made next and installed.

This was followed by adding the third layer. A problem noted earlier was made evident when the ball drop (?) was added. One spacer was designed to not allow the plastic sides to fully close. This also meant that the hanging part supported by the two plastic sides would not stay in position and would fall out of the holes. Installing the part was also an issue as the expanded end did not fit into the adjoining part. The interfering part is shown in the first photo below, pointed to by the small screwdriver. The spacer is too wide to fit in its mating hole. Balls rolling down this ramp fell into the holes but did not fall through, trapped by the hanging flaps below and not freed by a ball rolling down the bottom ramp. This obstruction was filed so the spacer now fits completely through the hole, seen in the second photo below.

Neither setup allows the balls to drop through the holes. With balls in the holes, supported by the underlying flaps, subsequent balls should drop in the last hole, hitting the flap and releasing the supported ball. Neither the unmodified setup (the designer's budgie job), nor the modified setup allowed this to happen. In both cases the flaps don't let the balls drop even when struck by a passing ball on the lower level.

The next four photos show the completed marble run from all four sides. The final photo is shot from above.

A mistake was located, the ramp for the balls leaving the wheels raising the marbles to the top, was assembled incorrectly. The small spacer should be on the top not the bottom as shown. This incorrect assembly means the ramp is not sufficiently sloped for the balls to always roll to the lower end. The part was easily removed and fixed, now working consistently.

There were many parts left over, fifteen not counting the sheet labeled spare parts or the parts labeled with a + after their number. This prompted me to check each of these extras to make sure none were left out. None had been omitted, much to my relief. Many of the extras were small easily broken pieces, a very generous bonus from ROKR.

In general the crank system works like a champ for raising the balls. The balls choose different paths through the marble run, so not all paths see equal action. This makes the run more interesting. The poor performance of, what I have called the marble drop, either in the original condition or the modified, is the only flaw noted, and it works on occasion. A very fun build and suitable for patient ten year-olds with supervision. A few assembly tasks were quite challenging requiring more force than a young child could provide. Definitely a winner and might inspire me to purchase ROKR's other marble run and the kit which bridges the two.
